Translucid

Visit Translucid.cloud ↗

Infrastructure for disposable cloud sessions and protected streaming.

Translucid gives AI screening companies, interview providers, and assessment platforms a controlled workspace inside their own product. A browser, IDE, terminal, or custom application runs in an isolated cloud session, with access, session policies, and protected streaming managed by the platform. Participants join through their browser; the working environment stays in the cloud.

Why it matters

An assessment can restrict its webpage while an AI assistant watches or listens from elsewhere on the same computer. Translucid moves the sensitive work into a disposable cloud environment and delivers its display through protected playback on supported devices. The platform gains control over where the work happens, without asking the participant to hand over control of their personal computer.

Why I started with assessments

Real-time AI tools made me question the assumptions behind online assessments. An assistant could interpret a screen or listen to a conversation from outside the application being monitored. Restricting what happened inside a browser tab could only address part of that problem.

I wanted to move the sensitive workspace into an environment the platform could control. The participant would still interact through a browser, but the actual browser, terminal, or assessment software would run in an isolated cloud session.

Translucid was my attempt to make that capability available to other companies. An assessment platform could keep its interface and content, then embed our protected workspace underneath it.

The work behind a responsive session

I spent roughly seven months working through cloud isolation, browser media security, and the lifecycle of remote sessions. Keyboard and mouse input had to reach the cloud machine, and the result had to come back quickly enough for someone to type, navigate, and work comfortably.

An ordinary remote desktop did not address the whole problem I was interested in. I explored DRM and browser Content Decryption Modules to protect the returned media on supported devices. That brought encryption, session keys, playback behavior, and operating-system differences into the same system.

A working demonstration was only one part of the job. Sessions needed to start reliably, survive interruptions, and shut down cleanly. A small delay that seemed acceptable while watching video could feel frustrating while editing code.

GPU capacity made startup time an infrastructure problem. A cold machine could need time to boot, load its image, initialize the graphics stack, and start the encoder before a user saw anything. Keeping a warm GPU pool could shorten that wait, but idle machines still cost money. The tradeoff was how much capacity to keep ready without paying for sessions nobody was using.

Custom VM images added another layer. An IDE, browser, or specialist application needed the right dependencies, display configuration, and runtime settings. An image that booted successfully was not necessarily ready for a low-latency interactive session. Provisioning, health checks, retries, and cleanup all had to agree on when a session was actually usable.

These were orchestration problems of the kind Kubernetes also addresses: scheduling work, reconciling desired and actual state, checking readiness, and reclaiming failed resources. For Translucid, the unit of work also included an isolated machine and a protected media pipeline. Getting compute ready was only the beginning; input routing, encoding, encryption, DRM licensing, and browser playback had to work together.

The difficult work often happened between those pieces. A healthy network connection did not necessarily mean smooth playback. A machine could be running while its encoder was still starting, leaving the user staring at an empty browser. I had to learn to judge readiness from the person using the session: could they open the terminal, type, and see the result? The services underneath could all look healthy before that was true.

Every use case asked for a different environment

The original idea expanded from a protected browser into custom workspaces. A programming interview might need an IDE. A practical certification could need particular command-line tools, software versions, or a configured environment.

These were plausible uses for the same foundation, but each came with another set of decisions: persistence, network restrictions, authentication, recording, and integration with an existing product.

I kept seeing possibilities across universities, recruiting platforms, certification providers, and sensitive remote work. It became harder to decide which experience we should make reliable first. Supporting the infrastructure was starting to look like supporting several different products.

What thirty-plus conversations changed

I spoke with more than thirty potential customers and people working around assessments and recruiting. I wanted to understand whether Translucid fit into the products they already had.

Those conversations made the cost of adoption much clearer. Someone considering infrastructure has to think beyond whether the demo works. If our machine failed to start, their interview failed to start. If our stream broke, their product looked broken. Adopting Translucid meant making our reliability, compatibility, and support part of their promise to their own customers.

As a small, unfamiliar company, we had to earn that trust while still proving there was a business. That was difficult. Engineering progress did not automatically make an integration easier for another team to justify.

I had started with friends, and some stepped away as the work continued. They did not see the same path forward, and I could not point to enough customer evidence to remove the uncertainty. I kept working, but I was asking myself many of the same questions.

Giving the technology a product of its own

I eventually decided to build an experience on top of the infrastructure myself. A meeting room was specific enough to explain and useful enough to try without first integrating a new platform into someone else’s application.

That became Qube: video, audio, and a shared cloud computer for sensitive demos and collaboration. The protected-session work stayed valuable, but the entry point changed. Someone could join a room and understand what it did.

This did not make the engineering simple or solve every security problem. Protection still depends on the device and playback environment. It gave me a clearer product boundary and a direct experience to improve.

What those months were worth

I sometimes wish I had tested the adoption question earlier. I was drawn to the technical challenge, and it was easy to interpret another working component as progress toward a company.

The work was not wasted. Qube depends on things I learned and built through Translucid. But I now separate the value of a technology from the willingness of a particular customer to adopt it.

I still see uses for Translucid as standalone infrastructure. For now, I am concentrating on the meeting experience it made possible, with a more specific answer to who it is for and how they can start using it.
