Qube

Visit Qube.so ↗

A protected virtual room for high-stakes conversations and shared work.

Qube brings video, audio, files, and a shared cloud computer into one room. People join through their browsers to interview, demo a product, review documents, or work together. The sensitive workspace runs inside the room, with protected media delivery on supported devices. Everyone has a place to work together without passing the underlying files and applications from laptop to laptop.

Why it matters

An interview, product demo, or research review now happens alongside AI assistants, meeting bots, screen recorders, and browser extensions. Those tools can have access to the same screen and audio as the participant. Qube gives sensitive work a controlled place to happen, with a shared computer that keeps the workspace inside the room.

From infrastructure to a room

Qube started after I had spent roughly seven months building Translucid. I had gone deep into isolated cloud sessions, remote input, low-latency streaming, and protected media. I could put a working environment in the cloud and let someone use it through a browser.

Selling that infrastructure asked much more of a customer than trying a demo. Another company would have to integrate it, depend on the runtime, and support the experience inside its own product. More than thirty conversations made that adoption problem hard to ignore.

I started wondering what would happen if I built the experience myself. I wanted someone to understand the value before I explained the architecture. A meeting room gave me a familiar starting point: send a link, join, and talk.

But the work inside a normal meeting still lives somewhere else. One person shares a screen. Another downloads a document. Someone opens a repository on their laptop. The call connects the people, but it has no workspace of its own. That became the idea behind Qube.

Putting a computer inside the meeting

The first decision was to put a computer in the room. That became QComputer. Files open there, code runs there, and people can browse or review a document together. The workspace belongs to the room instead of arriving through one participant’s screen share.

If I want to review a financial model with someone, I can open it inside Qube. If I want to show an unreleased product, I can run it on QComputer. An engineering interview can use a real IDE in the shared environment, without asking the candidate to clone a private repository onto their laptop.

That also gives access controls a clear place to apply. One participant can view the computer while another has keyboard and mouse control. A file can stay in the session without becoming a download on every participant’s device. For a disposable room, the temporary environment goes away when the session ends.

Protecting the whole interaction

Once the shared computer made sense, protecting only its display felt incomplete. An interview or security review also contains things people say out loud, show on camera, or explain while looking at a document. Video, audio, files, and the computer all belong to the same interaction.

That brought the Translucid media work back into the center of the product. Encrypting traffic in transit is only part of the problem. After ordinary media reaches a participant’s device, local recording tools and assistants may be able to access the output.

Qube keeps the working environment in the cloud and uses DRM-protected playback to keep the meeting stream away from ordinary local capture tools on supported devices. Fullscreen and focus controls can cover or pause the session when a participant switches away. The room has its own boundary, and leaving it becomes something the system can respond to.

Audio needed another layer. A protected stream can still leave through speakers and reach a microphone in the room. Qube’s acoustic leakage detection watches for that path, stops protected audio when leakage is detected, and can require headphones before continuing. For higher-assurance meetings, a paired second-device camera also gives the host a view around the participant’s workstation.

Forensic watermarking adds a way to investigate captured material. A subtle code identifies the session, so a recovered image carrying that mark can be traced back to the room it came from. It cannot stop someone pointing a phone at a display; it gives that capture a trail. Together, these controls address the digital stream, the surrounding room, and the evidence left afterward.

Making the room familiar and easy to use

A secure meeting still has to be a meeting people want to join. People expect to click a link, approve their camera and microphone, and start talking. They should not need to learn how a cloud session or DRM license works first.

I wanted the room to feel familiar from the first click: video tiles and call controls beside a browser with tabs, an address bar, and a place for shared files. People can open documents, spreadsheets, slides, drawings, or code without leaving the conversation. The interaction should feel like meeting in Zoom and working in Chrome, with the shared workspace inside Qube.

Underneath that simple interaction, several things have to happen together. Video and audio need to reach the right participants. QComputer needs responsive input. The workspace needs to start quickly, recover from interruptions, and clean up when the session ends. A delay that looks small in a service log can feel enormous when someone is waiting to type.

Translucid had often led me to ask whether each piece of infrastructure worked. Qube makes me ask whether someone can finish their meeting comfortably. If they can open a file, work together, and leave without thinking about the machinery underneath, the engineering is doing its job.

Letting a room remember

Translucid’s original rhythm was straightforward: start a computer, use it, destroy it. Meetings introduced a practical complication. The same team might come back tomorrow and want its files, browser tabs, and signed-in tools waiting.

Persistent Qube lets the room keep that continuity while the computer itself remains replaceable. With persistence enabled, the browser profile, workspace, and downloads are saved in an encrypted snapshot and restored into a fresh computer. The state belongs to the room, so people can return to their work without depending on one machine staying alive.

That made the lifecycle more demanding. A failed save must not replace the last good snapshot. Restoring one room must never expose another room’s files. Turning persistence off needs to delete the saved state. Those details are what make “pick up where we left off” possible without turning a temporary cloud computer into a permanent workstation.

Finding the first real use case

A protected room could fit interviews, product demos, security reviews, research, or sensitive document review. I had already learned from Translucid how easy it was to mistake a long list of possible uses for a clear product direction.

Hiring keeps pulling me back. AI tools can listen, watch, transcribe, and suggest answers without being part of the interview platform. Trying to identify every new assistant means keeping up with whatever appears next week. Qube puts the interview workspace inside a room the company controls, while the candidate joins from their own browser.

Fitting the room into an existing workflow matters just as much as what happens inside it. For hiring, the experience I want is simple: keep the scheduling process, attach a Qube room to the interview, and send the candidate a link. The room should fit into the team’s day without making them rebuild the process around it.

A live customer security review is another useful example. A vendor could open evidence on QComputer, explain it, and answer questions in the same place, without emailing every artifact beforehand. Both workflows need people to inspect something sensitive together. I am still testing where that need is strong enough to change how they work.

What I am still learning

Qube is still being built. I am learning which workflows matter most, which protections people understand, and where the room creates too much friction. There is no tidy retrospective ending yet.

The lesson from Translucid follows me into those decisions. I can spend weeks reducing latency or improving a security boundary because the problem is interesting and measurable. That does not automatically make it the next thing a user needs.

I want someone to arrive with a sensitive conversation or a piece of work and immediately understand why they would bring it into Qube. The infrastructure gives me the tools. Building the room is teaching me how to make those tools useful to someone else.
