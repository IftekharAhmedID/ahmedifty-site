Past work · By Iftekhar Ahmed (Ifty)

Diagnos

Personalized health intelligence built from the signals your body already produces.

Diagnos connected Intake, Recovery, Activity, and Vitals in one stream, bringing food logs, wearable data, and personal health check-ins together. It started with photo-based nutrition tracking at Rice and grew into a broader health app built around a person’s goals and daily habits. The aim was to turn those connected signals into useful, personalized recommendations.

Why it matters

Food, sleep, activity, and recovery affect one another, but the apps tracking them often show separate pieces of the picture. Diagnos brought those signals together so recommendations could account for more of a person’s daily life. Nutrition gave us a practical starting point: help students understand a meal from a photo and choose what to eat next.

A bigger idea than our first product

Diagnos began in a dorm at Rice in early 2024. There were five of us, coming from different backgrounds and majors, with a shared goal: make personalized healthcare accessible to everyone.

We were students trying to build around classes, with different ways of approaching the same problem. That mix shaped our early conversations. We wanted something people could use in everyday life, even if they could not afford a specialist for every question.

Phones and wearables were already collecting useful information, but food, sleep, activity, and recovery still lived in separate places. Our ambition was to bring those signals together and make them understandable enough to help someone decide what to do next.

I wanted to understand those signals together. A dashboard could tell someone how much they slept or ate, but what should they do with that information tomorrow? That was the question behind Diagnos.

At first, we explored much more than we could reasonably build. Rice gave us a practical place to begin: students ate in the same dining halls, and we could talk to them about a specific daily problem. We chose nutrition.

Making lunch easier to log

The first experience was straightforward. Take a photo of a meal and receive an estimated breakdown of its nutrients. We used the foods available in Rice’s serveries as context, rather than asking the model to identify every plate without knowing where it came from.

We worked on dining menus, meal histories, portion estimates, nutrition scores, and suggestions based on personal goals. A student should be able to understand what they had eaten and choose a useful next meal from food actually available nearby.

My work crossed design, algorithms, backend development, and the product itself. The early screens show how much we were trying to fit in: goals, daily summaries, menus, scanning, and trends. Even before launch, the scope was growing.

People started using it

We launched the nutrition app in September 2024. It spread through friends, student networks, campus outreach, and word of mouth. Our records eventually passed 1,000 users, with more than 100 returning in a week.

Those were encouraging numbers for something we had built alongside classes. They were also early numbers. Signups did not tell us whether we had a sustainable business, and we still needed to understand why people returned or stopped using it.

The Rice Thresher covered the launch that October. Seeing the app in the newspaper made the work feel less like an idea we kept explaining and more like something people expected to work.

A real task in a nutrition class

One of the most useful experiences came through Roberta Anding’s HEAL 103 nutrition course. Students used Diagnos for food logging and dietary evaluation. A seven-day diary activity also gave us structured feedback on how the app worked in everyday use.

This was a different kind of test from asking a friend to try a demo. Students needed to record meals over time and get information they could use in coursework. Confusing screens and tedious steps mattered immediately.

Looking back, that was a strong reason to keep improving the nutrition experience. We had a specific audience, a repeated task, and people who could tell us where it fell short.

The vision kept pulling us toward more features

Nutrition had always been the beginning of our health vision. Once the app had users, we connected all four major areas: Intake, Recovery, Activity, and Vitals. Food logs, wearable data, and subjective health check-ins could now feed into the same stream.

Each addition had a reason. Activity could help explain energy needs; sleep could put recovery in context. But measuring, processing, and interpreting all those signals well enough to give useful personalized recommendations went beyond what any of us knew at the time. We had connected the data, and then found ourselves struggling to make sense of it.

The burden on users grew too. They had to log meals, keep wearing their wearables, describe how they felt, and report how different foods affected them. Missing logs left gaps in the picture. The interface grew heavier, and tracking everything became a chore. In pilot testing, the broader app lost the appeal of the simple nutrition experience we had started with.

I had treated the ability to connect another signal as a reason to include it. We were learning how much a broad health app asks of someone every day. The more complete we tried to make the picture, the harder it became for a person to keep it up.

Rice Athletics changed the direction

Our conversations with Rice Athletics opened up a different use for the health data we had brought together. Coaches and support staff needed to understand athletes across nutrition, recovery, activity, and vitals. The individual app could become one part of a system for an entire team.

We impressed the director of Rice Athletics with what we were doing. Their excitement became ours, and we secured a six-figure deal for an Athlete Monitoring System. After struggling with the broader consumer experience, having an institution willing to buy felt like a clear direction. We shifted entirely toward athlete monitoring.

The athlete would still use the mobile app to log meals and contribute health data. But the person making decisions now sat on the other side of a dashboard. We were building for coaches, nutritionists, trainers, and staff who needed to review patterns across a group of athletes.

That changed the questions the product needed to answer. We had started with a student asking what to eat next. Now we were organizing player records, comparing trends, and building a Command Box where staff could ask questions across the data. The work expanded into reporting, integrations, and institutional workflows.

The old website captures how far we had moved. Its pitch centered on collegiate athletics, staff analytics, and a connected mobile-and-desktop platform. Some of the underlying technology carried over, but the customer, daily workflow, and company we needed to build had changed.

Our Spring 2025 application described the athletic-wellness beta as still in development. The nutrition app’s earlier users were evidence for that first product. The Rice deal gave us a customer for the new direction, but we still had to learn the market and deliver what we had promised.

Screenshots from the archived Diagnos website. Its claims about prediction and injury prevention describe the pitch at the time, not demonstrated clinical outcomes.

The decision that ended Diagnos

We let the excitement of the deal get ahead of our understanding of the market. We had not spent enough time studying Athlete Monitoring Systems, what teams already used, or how established platforms such as Teamworks fit into their operations. We committed before we understood what competing there would ask of us.

The team was already tired. The broader health app had stretched our knowledge and made it harder to see a clear path. The athletics opportunity gave us a burst of energy, but it also moved us into work we knew little about and had not come together to pursue.

We had started with an interest in personal health: helping someone understand their own body and make everyday decisions. Now we were building institutional software around athlete monitoring. As the work became clearer, we realized how far it was from the interests and passion that had brought us together.

Even with the six-figure Rice deal, we broke apart. A customer could give us a reason to deliver, but the deal could not make the team want to spend years building that company. The pivot became the decision that ended Diagnos.

That was the death of Diagnos.

What I would do differently

I would start with nutrition again. I would spend longer learning what made people return, where logging became annoying, and which suggestions changed a decision. We had enough early use to ask those questions seriously.

I would also make a change of customer an explicit decision. Before committing to athletics, I would study how teams already worked, what existing platforms did well, and where we had a specific problem worth solving. An excited buyer was a reason to investigate carefully. We treated it as a reason to move everything.

The team needed its own conversation before the commitment. Did we understand the work? Did we still have the energy for it? Did we actually want to build an athlete-monitoring company? We were so excited to have a deal that we did not give those questions enough weight.

I learned that commercial interest and team conviction have to meet. The size of a deal cannot settle whether the people building the product care about the work ahead. Changing the customer can change the company, even when much of the code survives.

Diagnos ran from early 2024 into 2025 and taught me how different building for real users feels from building toward a vision. I still care about the original health idea. What I carry into Qube is a stronger obligation to keep the immediate product understandable and useful.
