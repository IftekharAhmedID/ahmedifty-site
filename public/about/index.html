Journey

I started working on assistive technology when I was in seventh grade. I was trying to find a way for blind students to access the same textbooks as everyone else. That idea eventually grew into Brailly, where I worked on affordable Braille devices and software to make reading and using computers more accessible. Some of my early work appeared in the news under the name Iftekhar Ahmed Tonoy.

I didn't know much about human-computer interaction back then. I simply enjoyed building things and seeing how they made a difference in someone's life. Over time, I became more interested in the experience of using technology itself. How a small change in an interface might make something easier to understand, or allow someone to do something they couldn't before. That interest eventually led me toward human-computer interaction and later to my research on SwingBoard, a swipe-based virtual keyboard for blind and visually impaired users.

At Rice, I co-founded Diagnos with a student team. We started with the idea of making personalized healthcare more accessible, beginning with a nutrition app that helped students understand what they were eating through food photos and dining hall menus. We later began exploring health tracking with Rice Athletics. Working on Diagnos also taught me how easily a product becomes complicated when you start moving away from the problem people originally came to solve.

These days, I'm building Qube, which grew out of my earlier work on Translucid and isolated cloud computing. I became interested in how we might rethink online collaboration when people are working with sensitive information, and whether sharing a protected cloud computer instead of individual screens might offer a different experience.

Alongside that, I like exploring other questions through smaller projects. With Lenze, I'm interested in how AI understands what's happening in a visual environment as things change over time. With TraceDog, I'm exploring how we follow information back to its sources and understand what evidence supports a claim.

The problems have changed quite a bit since Brailly, but what interests me hasn't changed much. I enjoy understanding how people interact with technology, finding where existing tools fall short, and trying to build something that works better for them.
